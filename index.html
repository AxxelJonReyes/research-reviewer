Section 1 of 5

The Big Picture

Before we talk about instruments, we need to understand what we're actually trying to measure — and why it's harder than it looks.

Latent Construct vs. Observable Indicator

We rarely measure psychological or social variables directly. Instead, we infer them.

Latent ConstructObservable Indicator
Cannot be seen directly
e.g., stress, confidence, activation		 Visible evidence we can measure
e.g., survey responses, test scores, heart rate
Think of it like cooking: The latent construct is the invisible flavor of a soup. The observable indicators are the visible ingredients — salt, pepper, herbs.

What is a Research Instrument?

A specific, structured device used to extract observable data from your sample.

  • Questionnaires
  • Academic Tests
  • Observational Rubrics
  • Interview Guides
  • Performance Tasks
Key rule: Would you measure temperature with a ruler? Always use the right tool for the construct.

Garbage In = Garbage Out

Research flows in one direction:

Question → Construct → Variable → Instrument → Data → Analysis → Conclusion

Your conclusion is only as defensible as your measurement. Statistical analysis cannot save poorly measured data. Errors compound downstream.

Reliability vs. Validity — The Core Distinction

ReliabilityValidity
ConsistencyAccuracy
Same result every time?Measures what it should?
A measurement can be 100% consistent but 100% wrong. Example: a scale that always reads +5kg. Reliable? Yes. Valid? No.
Section 2 of 5

Validity

Validity asks: does the instrument actually measure what it claims to measure? Modern psychometrics treats validity as one unified argument, built from four kinds of evidence.

The Four Pillars of Validity

  1. Test Content — Does it cover the construct?
  2. Internal Structure — Do items relate properly?
  3. Response Processes — Do respondents interpret items as intended?
  4. Relations to Other Variables — Do scores correlate as theory predicts?
Validity is not a checklist. It's an evidence-based argument for how you interpret scores.

Face Validity

Does the instrument appear appropriate? Surface judgment of clarity and relevance.

Useful as a first check, but insufficient by itself. Ask: would "looks okay" convince a peer reviewer?

Content Validity

Did we adequately represent the construct?

Example: if you're measuring "Academic Stress," your instrument should cover workload, deadlines, assessment pressure, and time management — not just one of these.

  • Requires expert review and domain sampling
  • Quantified via Lawshe's CVR and Item-level CVI

Construct-Related Evidence

Do scores behave the way theory predicts they should?

Convergent EvidenceDiscriminant Evidence
Measures that should relate, do relate Dissimilar constructs remain distinct
Expected relationships depend entirely on theory.
Section 3 of 5

Reliability

Reliability asks: would the instrument give the same result if we used it again? It can break at four points — time, form, rater, or items.

1. Test-Retest (Consistency Over Time)

Same test → same group → two different occasions.

Best for: stable constructs like intelligence or personality traits.

Too short a gap = memory effect. Too long = the trait itself may have changed.

2. Parallel-Forms (Consistency Across Versions)

Two equivalent forms (Form A = Form B) — equally difficult, measuring the same concepts.

Best for: preventing testing threats like cheating or memory in pre/post designs.

Creating two truly equivalent forms is difficult, expensive, and time-consuming.

3. Inter-Rater (Consistency Across Raters)

Degree of agreement between two or more raters, judges, or observers.

Best for: observational research, performance evaluations, anything involving subjective judgment.

Requires clear, objective criteria and rigorous rater training.

4. Internal Consistency (Consistency Across Items)

How well individual items on a single test relate to one another.

Best for: multi-item scales like personality tests, attitude surveys, satisfaction questionnaires.

Can be artificially inflated by adding very similar or redundant questions.

Quick Match Guide

Clue in the scenarioReliability type
"Same test… different occasions"Test-Retest
"Two equivalent booklets/versions"Parallel-Forms
"Two independent coders/raters"Inter-Rater
"10 questions about 'grit' answered similarly"Internal Consistency

Three Paths to an Instrument

When you need an instrument, you have three options: adopt an existing one, adapt one to fit your context, or devise a new one from scratch.

The Three Paths

PathWhen to useAction
AdoptInstrument exists and fits your contextUse as-is
AdaptGood instrument, but needs modificationTranslate, tweak, adjust
DeviseNo suitable instrument existsBuild from scratch

The Decision Tree

Does a suitable instrument exist?
↓
No → Devise
Yes → Does it fit your population & context?
↓
No → Adapt
Yes → Adopt
Both Adapt and Devise require new psychometric evidence. You cannot assume the original validity carries over.

Critical Warnings

Misconception: "It was published, so it's automatically valid for us." A rigorously validated instrument for one population may not fit yours (different culture, age, context).
Re-evaluation rule: If you change the translation, examples, or response scale — none of the original psychometric evidence can be assumed. It must be re-evaluated.

Devising Requires a Blueprint

You cannot build a survey without defining the construct. Every question must trace back to the blueprint.

  1. Define Construct
  2. Identify Dimensions
  3. Map Indicators

  5. Expert Review & Pilot

Survey Flaws

Bad wording is measurement error. Every time a respondent is confused, random noise enters your data. Learn to diagnose and fix the six most common flaws.

The Measurement Trap Checklist

  • Double Negatives — "I do not believe AI is unhelpful."
  • Jargon / Acronyms — "I utilize LLMs for RAG workflows."
  • Unwarranted Assumptions — "How much did you study?"
  • Absolute Words — "I always finish on time."
  • Recall Problems — "How many hours did you sleep on a Tuesday 3 months ago?"
  • Social Desirability — Phrasing that makes respondents look good rather than tell the truth.
If they have to guess what you mean, your data is already compromised.

The Question Clinic: Diagnose → Explain → Repair

Flaw 1: Double-Barreled Questions

Asking two things in a single question.

"My research teacher explains clearly and gives helpful feedback."

Effect: Bimodal or flat distribution — respondents are answering different halves.

Split into two items: (1) "My research teacher explains concepts clearly." (2) "My research teacher provides helpful feedback."

Flaw 2: Vague Wording

Subjective terms mean different things to different people.

"I regularly use AI."

Effect: Injects noise — does "regularly" mean daily? Weekly? Monthly?

"During the past 7 days, on how many days did you use generative AI for schoolwork?"

Flaw 3: Broken Response Choices

Categories must be mutually exclusive (no overlaps) and exhaustively inclusive (cover all possibilities).

15–17, 17–19, 19–21

Effect: Impossible coding — where does a 17-year-old go?

15–16, 17–18, 19–20. No overlaps, all ages covered.

Scenario 2. The DCAT board develops Version X and Version Y — two distinct but equivalent test booklets. A sample group takes both to ensure they're interchangeable.

Scenario 3. A researcher uses two independent coders to analyze interview transcripts using the UTAUT framework. They check if coders assign the same codes.

Scenario 4. Checking if 10 questions about "grit" are answered similarly by the same student at the same time.

Scenario 5. Two clinicians diagnose patients using the same manual. Form A is given on Tuesday, Form B on Thursday.

Scenario 6. A personality test is taken in January and again in March to check stability.
